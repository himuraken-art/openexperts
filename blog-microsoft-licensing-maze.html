Microsoft 365 Licensing:
A Plain-English Guide to Not Overpaying

Microsoft makes their licensing intentionally complicated. We're going to un-complicate it — so you can stop paying for plans your team doesn't actually need.

I want to start with a confession: even after 30+ years in IT, I still have to look up the Microsoft 365 licensing comparison chart sometimes. Not because I don't know what each plan includes — I do — but because Microsoft quietly changes features, splits things into add-ons, and renames tiers often enough to keep everyone on their toes.

And that confusion isn't accidental. The more confusing the licensing, the more likely businesses default to "just give everyone the most expensive plan" to avoid thinking about it. We've seen small businesses with 15 employees paying for Microsoft 365 Business Premium when half their staff only check email and use a shared Word document a couple of times a week. That's not a small difference in price — it's real money walking out the door every month.

This guide is our attempt to cut through the noise. We'll explain the three main plans small businesses choose between, who actually needs each one, the most common overspending mistakes we see, and how to figure out what you're really using. No upsell agenda here — sometimes the right answer is a less expensive plan.

The Basics

The Three Plans Small Businesses Actually Choose Between

Microsoft offers a dizzying number of licensing options, but for small businesses with fewer than 300 users, you're almost always looking at one of three plans in the Microsoft 365 Business family. Here's the plain-English version of what each includes:

Microsoft 365 Business Basic (~$6/user/month)

This is the entry-level plan. It gives you Exchange email, 1 TB of OneDrive storage, Teams (the messaging and video calling app), and the web-only versions of Office apps — meaning Word, Excel, and PowerPoint run in a browser, not as installed desktop programs. You also get SharePoint for shared files and some basic security features.

What it doesn't include: installed desktop apps. Users work in a browser only. No Outlook desktop client, no locally installed Word, no locally installed Excel.

Microsoft 365 Business Standard (~$12.50/user/month)

This is the most common plan for small businesses. It includes everything in Basic, plus the full desktop Office suite — Outlook, Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Publisher, Access — installed on up to 5 devices per user. You also get some additional features like webinar hosting and customer scheduling tools.

Most businesses land here because their staff genuinely needs desktop Office apps. If your people are doing real work in Excel or creating complex Word documents, they need the installed version. The browser-based apps are fine for light work, but they're not the same.

Microsoft 365 Business Premium (~$22/user/month)

This is the full security-focused plan. It includes everything in Standard, plus a robust set of security and compliance tools: Microsoft Defender for Business (endpoint protection), Intune (device management), Azure AD Premium (advanced identity features like conditional access policies), and information protection tools.

The jump from Standard to Premium is significant — about $9.50 per user per month. On a 20-person team, that's roughly $190/month or $2,280/year extra. That's not nothing. But for businesses with genuine security or compliance needs, the features are worth it.

Matching Plans to People

Who Actually Needs Which Plan

Here's the part most Microsoft resellers won't tell you: not everyone in your company needs the same plan. Microsoft lets you mix and match licenses on the same tenant. That means your power users can be on Premium while your part-time receptionist is on Basic — and you pay accordingly.

Business Basic is usually right for...

  • Employees who mainly use email and Teams for communication
  • Staff who access shared documents occasionally but don't create complex files
  • Part-time or seasonal employees with light needs
  • Businesses whose primary productivity software isn't Office (accounting firms using specialized software, for example, may just need email)

Business Standard is usually right for...

  • Office workers who spend meaningful time in Word, Excel, or Outlook desktop
  • Staff who regularly create or edit complex documents and spreadsheets
  • Anyone who needs Outlook desktop with full calendar and contact sync
  • Most knowledge workers — this is where the majority of small business employees should land

Business Premium is usually right for...

  • Businesses with compliance requirements (HIPAA, PCI, legal, financial services)
  • Companies that need centralized device management (enrolling and managing laptops through Intune)
  • Organizations with remote or hybrid workers where endpoint security is a priority
  • Businesses that have been told by their cyber insurance provider to implement specific controls
  • Any business that's had a security incident and needs to tighten up quickly

The honest take: Premium is genuinely powerful and worth it when you need what it includes. But many small businesses buy it because a reseller said "it's the best one" — not because they actually use Intune or Defender. If those tools aren't being configured and maintained, you're paying for a gym membership you never visit.

Where the Money Goes

The Most Common Overspending Mistakes We See

After auditing Microsoft 365 environments for dozens of small businesses, the same mistakes come up over and over. Here are the big ones:

Everyone Gets the Same (Expensive) Plan

It's easier to just assign one plan to everyone. No decisions to make, no confusion about who gets what. But "easier" costs money. We regularly find companies where the owner, the accountant, and the office manager are on Premium — but the two employees who do primarily physical work and just need to check email are also on Premium. Mix and match. You're allowed to.

Ghost Licenses for Departed Employees

This one is shockingly common. We've walked into businesses paying for 22 licenses when they only have 18 employees — because nobody removed the accounts when people left. If someone departs, their Microsoft 365 license should be reclaimed and reassigned or removed. Every unconverted ghost license is money straight to Microsoft for nothing.

This is directly related to having a solid employee offboarding process. It's not just a security issue — it's a budget issue too.

Paying for Add-Ons Already Included in the Plan

Microsoft has a bad habit of creating standalone products that overlap with features already in their business plans. We've seen businesses paying separately for Microsoft Defender when they already have it included in Business Premium. Same with certain Teams add-ons and some backup tools. Before adding any Microsoft add-on, check whether it's already included in what you're paying for.

Buying Premium for the Security Tools but Not Using Them

This is the most painful one to see. Business Premium's value comes from actually configuring and using Intune, Defender, and the conditional access policies. We've audited tenants on Premium where Intune wasn't configured, Defender was in default mode, and no conditional access policies existed. At that point you've bought Standard and paid Premium prices. Either use the tools or drop the plan.

Ignoring the Microsoft 365 Apps for Business Plan

Most people don't know this plan exists. Microsoft 365 Apps for Business (~$8.25/user/month) gives you the full installed Office suite on up to 5 devices — but no Exchange email. If you're already using a separate email solution (Google Workspace, for example) and just need Office apps, this plan is significantly cheaper than Business Standard. It's a narrow use case, but if it fits, it's worth knowing about.

What to Actually Do

How to Right-Size Your Microsoft 365 Licensing

Here's a practical approach to figuring out whether you're over- or under-licensed. You don't need a consultant for the first part — you can do this yourself.

Step 1: Pull Your Current License Inventory

Log into the Microsoft 365 Admin Center at admin.microsoft.com. Go to Billing → Your Products to see what plans you're paying for and how many of each. Then go to Users → Active Users to see which license is assigned to whom. Cross-reference this with your actual headcount. Are there licenses assigned to people who no longer work there?

Step 2: Categorize Your Users

Go through your staff list and put each person in one of three buckets:

  • Light users: Primarily email and Teams, minimal Office use. Candidate for Basic.
  • Standard users: Regular use of Outlook, Word, Excel. Most of your staff probably lands here. Standard is the right call.
  • Power/compliance users: Need device management, advanced security, compliance tools, or handle sensitive regulated data. Premium makes sense.

Step 3: Check What You're Actually Using

In the Microsoft 365 Admin Center, go to Reports → Usage. This shows you which apps each user is actually opening. If you've assigned Premium to your team but the usage report shows nobody has touched Intune, that's a flag. Either get those tools configured (and get the value you're paying for) or reconsider whether Premium is the right tier.

Step 4: Check Your Security Configuration

If you're on Business Premium, make sure you're actually getting the security value. Are the key security settings configured? Is MFA enabled for all users? Is Defender for Business set up and monitored? If none of that is happening, you have a gap — either in how the plan is being managed, or in whether Premium is right for your situation.

Step 5: Look at Your Renewal Date and Plan Changes

Microsoft lets you downgrade licenses at your annual renewal. If you're on annual billing, make a note to review your licensing 30-60 days before renewal. You can add licenses anytime, but downgrading usually takes effect at renewal. Don't let auto-renewal lock you into another year of the wrong configuration.

The Numbers

What Right-Sizing Actually Saves

Let's make this concrete. Say you have 20 employees, and everyone is currently on Business Premium at $22/user/month. Your monthly bill: $440. Annual: $5,280.

After an honest audit, you find:

  • 2 employees left in the past year and their licenses were never removed
  • 5 employees are warehouse workers who just need email and occasionally read a shared schedule — Business Basic is fine for them
  • 11 employees are office staff who need full Outlook and Office desktop — Business Standard
  • 2 employees are the IT admin and the HR manager who handle sensitive data — Business Premium makes sense

New monthly cost: 5 × $6 + 11 × $12.50 + 2 × $22 = $30 + $137.50 + $44 = $211.50/month. Annual: $2,538.

That's roughly $2,742 per year back in your pocket just from right-sizing and removing two ghost licenses. For a small business, that's real money.

Your numbers will be different. But the point stands: a 30-minute audit can generate meaningful savings, and it's worth doing at least once a year.

One More Thing

Don't Cut Security to Save Money

We want to be clear about one thing: if your business genuinely needs the security features in Business Premium, don't cut the plan just to save a few dollars a month. The cost of a ransomware attack, a data breach, or a compliance violation will dwarf whatever you save on licensing.

The goal of this guide isn't to convince you to spend less — it's to make sure you're spending on what you actually need and using what you're paying for. If your business has compliance obligations, remote workers handling sensitive data, or a history of security incidents, Premium might genuinely be the right call for most or all of your team. That's fine. Just make sure those security tools are actually configured and being monitored, otherwise you're paying full price for half the value.
