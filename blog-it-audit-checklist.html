A 20-Point IT Audit You Can Do Yourself
(Before Calling Anyone)

You don’t need an IT company to tell you where your technology stands. Here’s a plain-English self-assessment that takes less than 10 minutes — and gives you a real picture of your risks.

Before you call an IT provider, before you spend a dollar, it helps to know what you’re dealing with. That way you’re not walking into a conversation blind — and you’re not dependent on someone else to tell you where your gaps are.

This isn’t a gotcha list designed to scare you into hiring anyone. It’s the same kind of checklist we use when we first sit down with a new client — 20 yes/no questions across five categories: backups, security, documentation, hardware, and disaster readiness. Answer honestly. Count your “yes” answers. Then use the scoring at the end to understand where you actually stand.

Some of this might be uncomfortable. That’s okay. Better to know now than to find out during a crisis.

How to Use This Checklist

Go through each question and answer yes or no. A “yes” means the thing is actually in place — not “I think so” or “probably.” If you have to guess, mark it “no” for now. Ambiguity counts as a gap. At the end, tally your “yes” answers and check the scoring section.

Category 1 — Questions 1–4

Backups

Backups are the most basic form of insurance your business can have. These four questions tell you whether yours would actually hold up when you need them.

1. Are your backups running automatically, every day, without anyone having to remember?
Manual backups — the ones where someone has to plug in a drive or click a button — get skipped. Life gets busy. The backup “runs” for months without anyone noticing it stopped. If your backups require a human to initiate them, they’re not reliable.

2. Are your backups stored in at least two separate locations?
A backup on the same server as your data isn’t a real backup. If that server dies, burns, or floods, both are gone at once. The standard is at least two copies in separate locations — local for speed, offsite or cloud for disaster recovery. See our post on backup testing for more on this.

3. Have you successfully restored a file from backup in the last 90 days?
A backup you’ve never tested is a guess. Backup software can fail silently for months — running, logging success, and producing nothing that can actually be restored. The only way to know your backups work is to actually use them. Even a small test restore counts.

4. Does someone receive an alert when a backup fails?
Most backup tools send email notifications. If nobody is watching them, they might as well not exist. Confirm that failure alerts are configured and going to a monitored inbox — not an unread system account.

Category 2 — Questions 5–9

Security Basics

You don’t need an enterprise security budget to cover the basics. These five questions cover the controls that stop the vast majority of attacks on small businesses.

5. Is multi-factor authentication (MFA) enabled for email and remote access?
MFA — the second prompt after your password, usually a code on your phone — stops over 99% of automated credential attacks. If your team is using Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, or any remote access tool without it, you have a wide-open door. We’ve written a full explainer on rolling out MFA if you need it.

6. Are security updates applied to computers and servers within 30 days of release?
Most cyberattacks exploit known vulnerabilities — flaws that already have patches available. Delayed patching is one of the most common ways businesses get compromised. If you don’t have a process for keeping systems current, this is a priority fix.

7. Do you have a business-grade firewall (not just a consumer router) on your network?
The $60 router from a big-box store is not a firewall. It’s a traffic cop with no ability to inspect what’s actually passing through. A proper business firewall does deep packet inspection, blocks known malicious traffic, and gives you visibility into what’s happening on your network. If you’re not sure what you have, read our post on consumer routers vs. business firewalls.

8. Does every employee have their own individual login — no shared accounts?
Shared logins mean you can’t track who did what, you can’t disable one person’s access when they leave, and a single compromised password affects everyone who uses it. Each person gets their own account. No exceptions.

9. Have your employees received any cybersecurity awareness training in the last 12 months?
Technology can only do so much. The most common way businesses get breached is through a person clicking something they shouldn’t. Phishing simulations and basic security training dramatically reduce click rates. It doesn’t have to be expensive — even a one-hour annual session makes a measurable difference.

Category 3 — Questions 10–13

Documentation

Documentation is boring. It’s also what saves you when something goes wrong or someone leaves. These four questions measure your “bus factor” — how well you’d survive if the person who knows everything about your IT suddenly wasn’t available.

10. Do you have a written inventory of all your hardware, including purchase dates?
A simple spreadsheet listing your servers, computers, switches, and when you bought them is enough. Without it, you have no way to proactively budget for replacements or know when something is approaching end-of-life. See our post on hardware lifecycle planning for why this matters.

11. Are your admin credentials stored securely — not in someone’s head, a sticky note, or a shared spreadsheet?
Admin passwords for your servers, router, firewall, and cloud services need to be stored somewhere secure that more than one trusted person can access. A business password manager is the right tool for this. If those credentials exist only in one person’s memory, you’re one phone call away from being locked out of your own systems.

12. Do you know who registered your domain name, and can you access that account?
We’ve seen businesses lose control of their own domain because it was registered by a former IT person or vendor, tied to an email account nobody uses anymore. Your domain name is critical infrastructure. You should own it, control it, and have the login credentials in your possession.

13. If your IT person quit or became unreachable tomorrow, could someone else take over without starting from scratch?
This is the ultimate documentation test. If the answer is “no” or “it would be really hard,” you have a documentation problem. Everything that lives only in one person’s head is a liability. For more on this, read our post on IT documentation and the bus factor.

Category 4 — Questions 14–17

Hardware & Infrastructure

Old hardware is a hidden cost center. These four questions help you assess whether your physical infrastructure is a liability waiting to surface.

14. Is your server (if you have one) less than 7 years old?
After 5-6 years, server hard drives and components start failing at meaningfully higher rates. After 7, you’re running on borrowed time — and when it goes, it usually goes at the worst possible moment. If you’re not sure when your server was purchased, that’s a problem in itself (see question 10).

15. Is every server and network device (switch, firewall) plugged into a UPS — not directly into the wall?
Power blips and surges happen constantly, most too brief to notice. But your server notices. A momentary voltage spike can corrupt a database or take out a drive. A UPS (uninterruptible power supply) is a $150–$300 piece of equipment that can save tens of thousands in data recovery or hardware replacement.

16. Is your server or network equipment in a secure location with adequate airflow and controlled access?
A server stuffed in a closet with no ventilation, next to cleaning supplies, with the door propped open is not a server room — it’s a liability. Heat kills hardware. Dust kills hardware. And physical access to a server bypasses most of your security controls. We’ve written a whole post about the things we find in small business server closets.

17. Do you have a separate guest Wi-Fi network that’s isolated from your business network?
Letting customers, visitors, or personal devices onto the same Wi-Fi as your business data and servers is a risk you don’t need to take. A separate guest network — available on most business-grade access points — keeps visitors connected without giving them any path to your internal systems. This also applies to IoT devices like security cameras and smart TVs.

Category 5 — Questions 18–20

Licensing, Access & Disaster Readiness

The last three questions cover common gaps that don’t get attention until something goes wrong — a compliance audit, an employee departure, or an actual disaster.

18. Are all your software licenses current, documented, and properly assigned?
Running expired or improperly licensed software exposes you to compliance risk and, in some cases, legal liability. This includes Microsoft 365, any line-of-business software, and industry-specific tools. If you’re not sure what you’re licensed for, that’s worth a quick audit. Our guide to Microsoft 365 licensing is a good starting point for that piece of it.

19. When an employee leaves, are their accounts and access disabled the same day they depart?
This is one of the most commonly neglected security controls in small businesses. A former employee with active credentials — even a friendly departure — is an open door into your systems. Access should be disabled on the last day, before they leave the building. For a full checklist on this, see our post on employee onboarding and offboarding.

20. Do you have a written plan for what happens if your main server or internet connection goes down for a full business day?
This doesn’t need to be a 50-page document. It just needs to exist. Who gets called? What systems can staff access from home or on mobile? What’s the fallback for taking payments, handling customer calls, and keeping the business moving? If the answer to any of that is “we’d figure it out”, you don’t have a plan — you have a hope.

The Results

How to Score Yourself

Count every question you answered “yes” to. Here’s what the numbers mean:

18–20: You’re in good shape.
Your IT foundation is solid. The gaps you have are small and manageable. Focus on closing the remaining “no” answers one at a time — you’re not in crisis mode, but there’s no such thing as done when it comes to IT.

14–17: Decent, but meaningful gaps.
You’ve got some real risks in the mix. Look at which category has the most “no” answers — that’s where to focus first. Backups and security gaps tend to have the highest consequences, so if those are where you’re missing points, start there.

10–13: Real exposure.
You have multiple gaps that could combine into a serious problem. It’s not a matter of if something goes wrong, but when — and you want to be ready. Use the category breakdowns to build a priority list and work through it systematically. Many of these fixes are free or low-cost.

Under 10: High risk. This needs attention.
Not to be alarmist, but a score below 10 means your business is running with significant, compounding vulnerabilities. You’re exposed on multiple fronts — a ransomware hit, a hardware failure, an employee departure, or any number of common incidents could cause serious damage. The good news is that most of this is fixable, and you can prioritize.

What to Do With Your Score

Whatever your number, the goal is the same: pick the two or three most critical “no” answers and start there. You don’t have to fix everything at once. Prioritize by consequence — a gap that could cause permanent data loss or a week of downtime ranks higher than one that’s inconvenient but recoverable.

For most small businesses, the highest-consequence gaps are: untested backups, no MFA, and no DR plan. If those three are red on your list, start there before anything else.
