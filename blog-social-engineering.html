Your Employees Will Give Away the Keys to the Kingdom
If You Ask Nicely Enough

The most sophisticated threat to your business doesn't come through a firewall. It walks in through the front door, calls your receptionist, or sends a friendly email. Here’s how social engineering really works — and what actually stops it.

We spend a lot of time talking about firewalls, antivirus software, and multi-factor authentication. All of that matters. But here’s a truth that the security industry doesn’t love to advertise: none of those tools stop the attack that starts with a phone call.

"Hi, this is Mike from IT support. We’re seeing some unusual activity on your account. I just need to verify your password real quick so I can lock it down before anything happens."

Would your receptionist hand over that password? Your bookkeeper? Your office manager who’s juggling three things at once and just wants to be helpful?

Probably yes. And that’s not because they’re careless or foolish. It’s because they’re human. Social engineering attacks are designed to exploit the things that make us good employees: being cooperative, wanting to help, trusting authority, and not wanting to cause trouble.

After thirty years in this industry, the attacks I’ve seen cause the most damage aren’t the sophisticated technical ones. They’re the ones where someone picked up the phone and asked politely.

The Basics

What Social Engineering Actually Means

Social engineering is the art of manipulating people into handing over information or access they shouldn’t give away. No malware required. No vulnerability to patch. The attacker’s primary tool is psychology.

The term covers a wide range of tactics, but they all share a common thread: they exploit human tendencies rather than technical ones. Things like:

  • Authority bias — we tend to comply when someone sounds like they’re in charge
  • Urgency — when we’re told something needs to happen right now, we stop thinking critically
  • Reciprocity — if someone does us a favor or seems helpful, we want to help back
  • Fear of consequences — nobody wants to be responsible for a security incident or miss something important
  • Social proof — "everyone else in accounting already updated their credentials"

The best social engineers understand these triggers better than most therapists. They build scenarios that make the most natural, polite response also the most dangerous one.

This is different from phishing, though there’s overlap. Phishing is typically mass-volume, sending the same fake email to thousands of people. Social engineering is often targeted — the attacker has done their homework on your business, your employees, and your systems before they ever make contact.

The Playbook

The Attacks You’re Most Likely to Face

Pretexting

This is when the attacker creates a fabricated scenario — a "pretext" — to establish trust and extract information. The most common version in small businesses: someone calls claiming to be from your IT vendor, Microsoft support, your bank’s fraud department, or a government agency.

They’ve often done reconnaissance first. They know your company name, the name of your IT provider (from your LinkedIn or a job posting), maybe even the name of your IT contact. That detail is what makes it convincing. "Hi, I’m calling from [your actual IT company] and I’m trying to reach someone about your account." Now you’re off-guard because they know your vendor’s name.

Vishing (Voice Phishing)

Phone-based attacks are surging because they’re cheap, effective, and hard to filter. Unlike email, there’s no spam folder for phone calls. AI voice cloning is making this worse — it’s now possible to generate a convincing imitation of a known voice from a few seconds of audio.

The classic scenario: a call to your accounts payable person claiming to be a vendor, asking to update their bank account for future payments. The FBI reports this exact attack — known as Business Email Compromise — costs American businesses billions of dollars a year.

Tailgating

This one is physical, not digital. Someone follows an authorized employee through a secured door without badging in themselves. The setup is usually low-tech: arms full of boxes, a coffee carrier, or just moving confidently and saying "can you hold that?" to the person ahead of them.

Once inside, they can plug a USB drive into an unattended machine, photograph documents left on desks, or access areas they have no business being in. For businesses with server rooms or areas with sensitive paperwork, this matters.

Impersonation

Someone shows up in person claiming to be from your copier company, your pest control service, the fire inspector, or the building’s IT team. They’re dressed the part, they have a clipboard, and they’re confident. Most front desk employees aren’t going to call the company to verify — they’ll just let them through.

Once inside, the game is similar to tailgating. The goal is usually either physical access to a machine or to get left alone long enough to do something they shouldn’t.

The Human Element

Why Even Smart, Careful People Fall for These Attacks

When a social engineering attack succeeds, the instinct is to blame the employee who fell for it. That’s the wrong lesson, and it’s counterproductive. Here’s why these attacks work on people who are otherwise sharp and responsible:

They’re designed by professionals. The people running targeted social engineering campaigns practice constantly. They test their scripts, refine their approaches, and get very good at reading responses and adjusting in real time. Your receptionist has never trained for this. That’s not a fair fight.

They exploit good instincts. Wanting to be helpful isn’t a flaw. Neither is respecting authority or not wanting to hold up someone’s workday. Attackers exploit the same qualities that make someone a good employee.

They create cognitive pressure. Urgency is the attacker’s best friend. "This needs to happen right now or your account will be locked." "I’m standing in front of your building and my keycard isn’t working, can you just buzz me in?" When people are rushed, the deliberative part of the brain takes a back seat and autopilot takes over. Autopilot says: be helpful.

They target the moments when people are busiest. A call that comes in at 4:45 on a Friday, right before a long weekend, when the person is trying to finish something up, is far more likely to succeed than the same call on a quiet Tuesday morning. Attackers know this.

We ran phishing simulations with clients and found that click rates spike around 3pm on Fridays and in the first 15 minutes after lunch. Social engineering calls follow the same pattern. The lesson isn’t that your employees are gullible — it’s that everyone has moments of lowered guard, and the people doing this for a living know when to strike.

Real Examples

How These Play Out at Small Businesses

These aren’t hypotheticals. These are variations on situations we’ve either responded to or heard directly from other business owners.

The IT helpdesk call. An employee gets a call from someone identifying themselves as Microsoft support. They say they’ve detected unusual sign-in activity on the employee’s Microsoft 365 account. They walk the employee through "confirming" their account by logging into a fake page, capturing the credentials in real time. Within an hour, the attacker is inside the account. The employee didn’t do anything wrong by their own understanding — they cooperated with what sounded like a legitimate security process.

The new vendor payment. A small manufacturing company gets an email and a follow-up call from someone pretending to be a materials supplier they’d worked with for years. The caller explained that they’d switched banks and needed to update their ACH information before the next invoice. The accounts payable person updated the records. The next $60,000 payment went to an account in another country. By the time the real vendor called asking about the late payment, the money was gone.

The office walk-in. A man in a polo shirt with a branded clipboard showed up at a dental office claiming to be doing a routine inspection of the network equipment. The front desk let him into the server closet. He spent 20 minutes in there, plugged a device into the network switch, and left. The practice discovered the breach three months later when patient data appeared on a dark web marketplace.

None of these businesses were run by careless people. They were run by people who hadn’t built the right systems and habits to catch this kind of attack.

The Fix

What Actually Makes a Difference

The good news: defending against social engineering doesn’t require expensive tools. It requires clear policies, practiced habits, and a culture where it’s okay to be skeptical.

Establish a verification protocol and actually use it

Your employees need a clear rule: before giving any sensitive information or access to anyone — over the phone, by email, or in person — verify who they are through an independent channel. Not the phone number they called you from. Not a callback to a number they give you. Call the number you already have on file, or look it up yourself.

Legitimate vendors, IT providers, and government agencies will never object to this. Only fraudsters will push back against verification. That reaction itself is a red flag.

Create explicit "never do this" rules

Some things should simply be non-negotiable:

  • We will never share passwords over the phone, period. Not to IT, not to Microsoft, not to anyone.
  • We will never wire money or change payment details based on a phone or email request alone — it requires a voice confirmation via a known number.
  • We will never let anyone unescorted into the server room or areas with sensitive equipment.
  • We will never plug in a USB device someone left for us or handed us.

Write these down. Post them somewhere visible. Make them part of onboarding. If you need a full checklist for new employee security basics, our onboarding/offboarding guide covers this in detail.

Train for the actual experience, not just the concept

Reading about social engineering is useful. But the real value comes from simulated practice — actually getting a fake call or email and having to respond to it. When people have been tested, even if they failed the first time, they develop a mental reflex that kicks in when the situation feels familiar.

There are low-cost services that can run phone and email social engineering simulations for your team. The goal isn’t to shame anyone who gets fooled. The goal is to build the experience so the reflex is there when it matters.

Make it safe to say no or to escalate

One of the most underrated things you can do as a business owner: explicitly tell your employees that it is always okay to slow down, ask for verification, or escalate to you before complying with an unusual request. Many employees go along with something suspicious because they’re afraid of being seen as obstructive or paranoid.

Remove that fear. Tell them directly: "If something feels off, I want you to pause and check with me. No request is so urgent that you can’t take two minutes to verify." That cultural shift costs nothing and prevents a lot of incidents.

Pair it with technical controls

Even with the best training, people occasionally slip. Technical controls provide a second line of defense. Multi-factor authentication means that even if an attacker gets someone’s password, they can’t get in without the second factor. Proper Microsoft 365 security settings can flag suspicious sign-ins and require additional verification from unusual locations. Payment verification workflows that require dual approval above a certain dollar amount stop the fraudulent wire transfer even when the person who answered the phone was fooled.

Defense in depth — multiple overlapping layers — is the right approach. Social engineering often wins when there’s only one line of defense and it gets bypassed. Stack the controls.

The Bigger Picture

Building a Culture of Healthy Skepticism

The goal isn’t to make your employees paranoid or to treat every caller as a threat. That would make your business miserable to work in. The goal is to build habits — standard, practiced, non-negotiable habits — around certain categories of requests.

Think about it like a physical security habit. You lock your front door when you leave. You don’t spend a lot of time agonizing over it; you just do it. That’s what we’re building here: a set of automatic behaviors that kick in when someone asks for access, credentials, money movement, or entry to secured areas.

The businesses I’ve seen handle this best share a few things. The owner talks about security openly and regularly — not in a fear-based way, but matter-of-factly. They treat a near-miss or a question about a suspicious call as a good thing, not an inconvenience. And they practice: they test their people, debrief without blame when someone almost fell for something, and use real near-misses as teaching moments.

Your technology is only as secure as the people who use it. The good news is that people, unlike software, can learn.
